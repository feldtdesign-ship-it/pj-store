The story

No investors.No connections.No permission.

A kid from Tulsa, Oklahoma dropped out of college, landed on a friend's couch in Astoria, and gave himself one month to figure New York out. This is how a Home Depot cart became a brand.

2012
Chapter one · The cart

The subway was the first gallery. Raw, public, unforgiving.

On September 26, 2012, PJ wheeled a cart he had built from a single Home Depot run, wired together with metal clips and chicken wire, onto an MTA platform and started selling original prints to strangers between trains.

There was no plan B. He worked out of a basement studio apartment, drew on the train, and sold on the platform. For eight years, that was the whole business: the artist, the art, and whoever was waiting for the L.

Proving demand the hardest way there is.

2014
Chapter two · DUMBO

A used heat press and a box of plastisol transfers. That was the clothing line.

Pop-ups under the bridge in DUMBO moved the work off paper and onto fabric. Prints became tees. Tees became a following. The designs were the same ones that had already sold underground, so nothing was a guess.

The cart kept running. The pop-ups were where the apparel got proven, one weekend at a time, by the same people who had been buying prints between trains.

2019
Chapter three · Storefronts

11th and First. Then 11th and Broadway. Then 182 Lafayette.

The first East Village storefront opened in 2019 at 11th Street and First Avenue, followed by a summer run at 11th and Broadway. In 2022 the shoppe moved to SoHo, at 182 Lafayette Street, for a Fall/Winter season that turned into a strong 2023 retail year.

Each room was small, temporary, and full. The East Village print, the neighborhood's name built entirely out of fire escapes, ladders, and railings, was drawn from those blocks.

C&D
Chapter four · The logo that got lawyered

New York Fuckery drew a cease and desist. The design did not move.

Drawn in part on the Union Square L, the uptown 6, and the 72nd Street Q platform, New York Fuckery is a satirical transit emblem: MetroCard, service-change signage, and a giant yellow wordmark about the daily absurdity of getting through the city.

It drew a cease and desist. PJ's legal defense held the line on parody and free expression, and the matter was resolved without the artist folding or pulling the design. Independent artist, real legal exposure, real outcome.

Held the line. Kept the design.

2025
Chapter five · Union Square to the Seaport

Back to selling direct. Then a storage space became a shoppe.

In 2024 PJ went back to selling direct at Union Square, then turned a reactivated storage space into a working shoppe. From January 2025 the brand ran recurring pop-ups at 31 East 17th Street, then opened at the Seaport, 229 Front Street, where the weekend crowds found it fast.

Small rooms, short runs, lines out the door. Every room proved the same thing the platform did: put the work in front of New York and New York wears it home. The flagship is next.

In the wild

Tyson vs. van Gogh, out in the world.

The work is built the same way the business was: out of what the city hands you. Subway systems, transit signage, tabloid headlines, and old New York iconography get stripped down to stencil and screenprint, then put back out into the world they came from.

Sometimes the world sends a picture back.

How it's made

Stencil. Screen. Ink. Sign.

Nothing here is printed on demand. Designs are cut as stencils, burned to screens, and pulled in the Bronx in short runs. Garments are hand-sewn in the East Village. Prints are signed by PJ, numbered, and the edition closes.

  • PrintPlastisol ink, hand-pulled, heavyweight cotton.
  • SewCut and sewn in the East Village. Bombers, vests, and coats built to be worn out.
  • SignTabloid, movie, and canvas prints signed by hand. Editions of 100, 25, and 5.
  • ShipPacked and shipped from the shoppe. Prints go flat, in a tube.
